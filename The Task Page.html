02Why these nine and not others

Every animation on the screen answers a question the athlete arrived with. Motion that answers nothing is the thing that makes an app feel cheap.

Their questionWhat moves to answer it
“How long have I got?”The ticking clock. A static “due today” label is ignorable; a second hand is not.
“What am I supposed to do?”The headline lifting into place before anything else settles. It arrives first and it arrives heaviest.
“Is anyone else actually doing this?”The live count with its breathing dot, the quote from yesterday, and the ticker. Three different answers to the same anxiety.
“Am I getting anywhere?”The dial drawing and the XP counting. Progress that animates in reads as progress you made; progress already drawn reads as a fact about you.
“Is this thing alive?”The brick flashes and the slow push on the photo. Neither carries information. Both stop the screen reading as a document.
The one to keep if you cut everything else

The countdown. It is six lines of JavaScript, it needs no design, and it changes the emotional register of the whole screen from a list of things to a thing that is happening. Every other animation here is a refinement; that one is load-bearing.

03Rules for motion on this screen

  1. Nothing moves that isn't answering a question. Decoration is how motion starts feeling like a loading screen. Every animation above maps to a line in §02.
  2. Nothing loops faster than four seconds. The sweep is 4.6s, the photo 22s, the ticker 26s. Anything quicker gets noticed, and anything noticed on the fifth visit is annoying by the tenth.
  3. One thing is allowed to shout, once. The approval toast. If the celebration also fires on open, on scroll, or on a pull-to-refresh, it stops being a celebration.
  4. The entrance runs once per session, not per navigation. Coming back from the Brief screen should not replay the whole open — set a flag and skip straight to the settled state.
  5. Transforms and opacity only. The one exception is the progress bar's width, which is cheap because it animates once. Nothing else touches layout.
  6. Everything degrades to still. prefers-reduced-motion lands every element in its final state with no transition. The screen loses nothing except the theatre.
  7. Time is local and honest. The countdown runs against the athlete's own clock in IST. A deadline that is wrong by a timezone is worse than no deadline at all.
